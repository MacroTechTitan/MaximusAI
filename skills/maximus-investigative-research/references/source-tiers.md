# Source tiers — primary / secondary / adversarial

Investigative reporting lives or dies on source quality. This reference maps the three tiers used throughout the skill.

## Primary sources — the entity's own outputs, or the record itself

These are what an investigation aims to reach. Citations from this tier are the strongest.

**Corporate / financial:**
- SEC filings (EDGAR) — 10-K, 10-Q, 8-K, S-1, Form 4, Form 13F, DEF 14A
- Companies House (UK), state incorporation filings (US), OpenCorporates
- USPTO patent filings, trademark filings
- Audited annual reports
- Official press releases from the entity's own newsroom
- Corporate SEC-registered communications

**Legal / regulatory:**
- Court filings on PACER (federal), state court databases
- Court opinions from official court sites
- Agency enforcement actions (FTC, SEC, DOJ press releases with docket numbers)
- Regulatory filings (FDA, EMA, FCC, EPA, etc.)
- Sanctions lists (OFAC, EU consolidated, UN, UK OFSI)

**Personal (public figures only):**
- FEC filings for political contributions
- SEC insider-trading filings (Forms 3, 4, 5)
- Court records where the person is a named party
- Published books, articles, interviews (on-the-record)

**Data / research:**
- Original datasets released by the source
- Peer-reviewed publications from journal sites (not summaries)
- Government statistical releases (BLS, Census, FRED, etc.)

## Secondary sources — reporting *about* primary sources

Necessary for context, sometimes the only available source. Weaker than primary; cite the primary if it exists.

**Reputable outlets (2026, US/global — not exhaustive):**
- Bloomberg, Reuters, WSJ, FT, NYT, WaPo, The Economist, AP
- The Guardian, The Atlantic, The New Yorker (long-form)
- Trade press for the specific vertical (e.g. STAT News for biotech, TechCrunch for startups, Politico for policy)
- ICIJ, OCCRP, ProPublica (investigative)

**Analyst reports:**
- Cite only when the analyst is named. Sell-side reports from anonymous "team" bylines are less trustworthy than named analysts with track records.

**Do NOT cite as a source (use only to navigate to the primary):**
- Wikipedia
- Aggregator sites (business summaries, "about" databases)
- Content-farm rewrites of original reporting

## Adversarial sources — sources with a known angle against the entity or claim

Read carefully. These are essential for the Contradiction Hunt phase, but they have motivation.

**Corporate / financial adversarial:**
- Competitor filings and press releases
- Litigation records where the entity is a defendant
- Short-seller reports (motivated but often well-researched)
- Whistleblower statements (documented — not anonymous forum posts)
- Consumer protection agency complaints (with docket numbers)

**Journalistic adversarial:**
- Outlets with a documented critical stance on the sector or entity
- Long-form critical profiles (approach as one perspective, not the truth)
- Fact-check outlets (Snopes, PolitiFact, Reuters Fact Check) — useful for context, not deference

**Structural:**
- Sanctions databases (OFAC, EU consolidated) — hits here are load-bearing
- Enforcement action press releases naming the entity
- Regulatory correspondence obtained via FOIA

## What counts as "independent" corroboration

Two sources are independent only if they did not obviously get the claim from the same upstream source.

**Not independent:**
- Two outlets both citing the same wire-service report
- Two blog posts both linking to the same tweet
- Two summaries of the same primary document

**Independent:**
- Two separate primary sources agreeing (e.g. SEC filing + court record)
- Two outlets with named reporters who conducted separate interviews
- A primary source plus reporting by an outlet that did independent verification

## Freshness note

Reputable-outlet lists shift. As of **2026-07-31**, the list above reflects current standards. Do not treat the list as canonical for 2027+ — reputation changes with ownership changes and editorial track records.
